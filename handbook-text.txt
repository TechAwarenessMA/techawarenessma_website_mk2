HOW TO USE THIS HANDBOOK
This isn't reading material. It's a course. Five modules,
each with a checkpoint you have to actually do,
then a written knowledge check and a certification
submission at the end.
Here's why it works this way: TAA is small and growing fast.
We can't personally train every chapter lead, and we're not
going to pretend we can. So we wrote down everything we
know and built a way to check that you actually learned it.
Steps 4 and 5 are real gates, and yes, we do turn
people away at them. Not because we enjoy being
difficult. It's the reason a library trusts a stranger
in their building under our name.
DON'T RUSH IT
If you try to knock this out in a weekend, you'll fail
the certification submission. The hands-on parts
need you to take devices apart and put them back
together over and over, and that takes days, not hours.

WHAT WE BELIEVE
01
A DEVICE YOU CANNOT OPEN IS A
DEVICE YOU DO NOT OWN
Manufacturers spent twenty years making
repair harder: glued batteries, weird screws,
serialized parts. That's power moving
away from ordinary people, and we teach
repair as a way of taking some of it back.
02
THE PERSON DOES THE REPAIR. NOT
US.
The hardest rule we have. If you leave
a workshop having personally fixed
twelve devices while twelve people
watched, you failed. We're a teaching
organization, not a repair counter.
03
FAILURE IS PART OF THE LESSON, AND
WE PLAN FOR IT
Someone will strip a screw. Someone
will tear a ribbon cable. Good. That's
what learning looks like, and it's
why we practice on devices we own
instead of people's real phones.
04
NOBODY GETS CONDESCENDED TO
Our attendees ran businesses, taught
school, wired houses, kept books. They're
capable adults who were never shown
this one particular thing. The second
your tone slips into baby talk, you've
lost them, and they won't come back.
05
BROKEN THINGS ARE A RESOURCE,
NOT GARBAGE
Around sixty million tons of e-waste gets
generated worldwide every year, and only
a fraction gets properly recycled. Every
device a chapter keeps alive is one that
never becomes trash, and every dead
one is teaching material or spare parts.
READ THIS FIRST
If any of these five bugs you, this isn't
the right organization for you. Better
to figure that out now, on this page.

TOOLS, WORKBENCH, AND SAFETY
Every serious repair failure comes down to one of three things: the wrong tool, a lost screw, or a battery.
THE KIT · IFIXIT PRO TECH TOOLKIT
64-bit driver
Phillips, Torx, Pentalobe, JIS, Tri-point and
more. Seat the bit fully, press down, turn
steadily.
Nylon
spudger ×2
Your go-to around connectors and boards.
Pointed end for cables, blunt end for prying.
Plastic
opening
tools ×3
The default pry. Not sure which tool to grab?
It's usually this one.
Opening
picks ×6
Cut and hold screen adhesive. They wear
out, so buy extras.
ESD
tweezers ×3
Screws, ribbon cables, tight spots. Genuinely

Suction cup Lifts glued displays. Warm the screen first;
tape over shattered glass.
Metal

Jimmy
For stubborn casings only. Not ESD safe.
These stay in the Lead's hands, not an
attendee's.
Wrist strap ·
magnetic
mat
The two most underestimated things in the
kit. More on both at right.
Buy separately: 91%+ isopropyl, lint-free swabs,
nitrile gloves, a good light, adhesive strips, a
multimeter, and a fireproof battery container.
SCREW DISCIPLINE · THE MAT METHOD
1 Draw the device outline on the mat before you
open anything
2 Each screw goes on the mat in the position it
came from
3 Circle and label each group: "back cover",
"shield", "bracket"
4 On reassembly, work backwards through the
mat
5 Can't account for every screw? Don't close the
device.
A screw one millimeter too long, driven
into the wrong hole, can punch straight
through a logic board. The mat gets
used or the repair doesn't start. Hand
attendees the marker; drawing the
outline turns them from a passenger
into the person running the process.
ESD · THE DAMAGE YOU CAN'T FEEL
You're carrying a static charge you can't feel,
and a zap way below anything you'd notice
can quietly damage a chip. The damage
often shows up later: the device works
today and dies in three weeks. In a teaching
setting, that's the worst possible outcome.
→ Wrist strap on, clipped to unpainted metal,
before the case opens
→ Touch grounded metal before handling any
board
→ No wool, fleece, or shuffling around on carpet in
socks
→ Boards go into antistatic bags or onto the mat,
never onto fabric
→ Metal spudger and Jimmy are not ESD safe;
nylon and ESD tweezers are
BITS YOU'LL ACTUALLY MEET:
PH000 TO PH1 · P2/P5 PENTALOBE · Y000 TRI-
POINT · T2 TO T8 TORX · JIS (STRIPS IF YOU
USE A PHILLIPS BIT ON IT)

BATTERY SAFETY: NO EXCEPTIONS, EVER
Puncture a lithium cell and it can vent superheated gas and catch fire. This is the one thing in the whole
curriculum that can put someone in a hospital.
THE SEVEN RULES
1 · Below 25% charge before any repair near
a battery. iFixit tested this: a punctured cell
at about 25% smoked and sparked but
never hit thermal runaway. A full cell is a
completely different event.
2 · Plastic tools only near a battery. Always.
No metal spudger, no Jimmy, no
screwdriver, no knife.
3 · Never pry against the face of a cell. Pull
the tabs, use isopropyl, use gentle heat, be
patient. A battery that won't come out is
information, not a challenge.
4 · A swollen battery ends the repair.
Bulging cover, lifting screen, a device that
won't sit flat. Full procedure at right.
5 · Punctured cell? Everyone backs away.
Forget the repair and the data. No water.
Don't breathe the gas. Fire beyond a quick
spark means evacuate and pull the alarm.
6 · Batteries never go in the trash. Tape the
terminals, into the container, off to a real
battery recycling drop-off. Lots of libraries
already have one. Ask.
7 · You are the last word. An attendee may
push you to keep going. Say no. This is the
one spot in this handbook where you
overrule the person who owns the device.
REQUIRED AT EVERY WORKSHOP: FIREPROOF
CONTAINER WITH A LID · NITRILE GLOVES ·
KNOW WHERE THE EXTINGUISHER IS · A ROOM
YOU CAN CLEAR FAST. NO CONTAINER, NO
BATTERY WORK.
THE SWOLLEN BATTERY PROCEDURE, EVERY SINGLE TIME
01 	Stop. Tools down.
02 	Don't press, bend, flex, or pry the device
03 	Power it off. Don't charge it.
04 	Into the fireproof container
05 	Tell the attendee straight: it's a fire risk, don't use or
charge it, it needs a professional. Hand them the
referral list.
06 	Log it in the post-workshop report
You'll feel like you're letting them down. You're not. You're
the person in the room who knew what they were looking at.

MODULE 2 	3H READING + 4H HANDS-ON 	METHOD BEATS MEMORIZATION
THE UNIVERSAL REPAIR SEQUENCE
Every repair on every device follows the same nine steps. Teach the sequence itself. It's worth more than any single
repair, because it transfers to devices we never covered.
01 · LISTEN
Let them describe the problem all the
way through without jumping in to guess.
"It's slow," "it shuts off," and "the screen
went dark" are three different problems.
02 · REPRODUCE
Make the fault happen in front of you. If you
can't reproduce it, you can't prove you fixed it.
03 · ASK THE BORING QUESTIONS
How old is it? What changed? Was it
dropped? Was it wet? And the single
best diagnostic question there is: "has
anyone worked on it before?"
04 · RULE OUT SOFTWARE
Reboot, check storage, battery health,
updates, and any app installed the
week it started. This step alone solves
a third of what walks in the door.
05 · RESEARCH
Look up the exact model on iFixit. Read
the whole guide first, and read the
comments, because that's where you
find out step 14 breaks the flex cable.
06 · PLAN THE EXIT
What's the failure mode of this repair,
and what do you do if it happens? If
there's data on it the owner cares about
and it isn't backed up, back it up or stop.
07 · DISASSEMBLE DELIBERATELY
Mat out, screws mapped, photos as you
go. Stop at the first weird resistance and
figure out why. Force isn't a technique.
It's a decision to break something.
08 · TEST BEFORE CLOSING
Power on and test the fault plus the basics:
display, touch, sound, charging, buttons.
Finding a dead speaker after you've re-glued
the screen means doing the repair twice.
09 · DEBRIEF
What was wrong, what fixed it, what to watch
for. If it was the battery, explain what battery
health means and how to slow the decline.
1/3 of "broken" devices are actually software: full
storage, a failed update, a rogue app, a setting.
Always rule out software before you open anything.
The highest satisfaction-per-minute repair in existence: a plug of pocket
lint packed into the charging port. Wooden toothpick, gentle scraping,
phone off, no metal. It's the perfect first win for a nervous attendee.

MODULE 3 	ABOUT 4 HOURS OF VIDEO 	WRITTEN GUIDES TEACH SEQUENCE. VIDEO TEACHES HANDS.
REQUIRED VIEWING
THE ACTIVE VIEWING PROTOCOL
Watching repair videos on the couch is entertainment.
It builds zero skill. Follow this or the module is wasted:
1 Watch once at normal speed, hands empty, phone
away
2 Watch again with a practice device in front of you,
pausing at every step and doing it
3 Write down every warning. A warning is the
compressed experience of someone who already
broke one.
4 Study how they talk. The good ones narrate their
reasoning, not just their actions. That's exactly the style
you'll use at a workshop table.
5 Watch their recoveries. Someone calmly fixing a
mistake on camera is the most valuable footage in the
whole video.
ASSIGNED CHANNELS. WATCH SEVERAL; ONE PRESENTER'S HABITS AREN'T THE WHOLE CRAFT.
Phone Repair Guru 	Your main phone channel. Beginner-friendly, full walkthroughs. ASSIGNED: 4
FULL REPAIRS: SCREEN, BATTERY, CHARGE PORT, ONE DIAGNOSTIC
iFixit 	Teardowns and tool technique. Pair each video with the written guide for the
same device. ASSIGNED: 2 TEARDOWNS OF DEVICES IN YOUR INVENTORY
Hugh Jeffreys 	Full restorations, smart parts sourcing, and the best explanation anywhere of
parts pairing and serialization. ASSIGNED: 2 RESTORATIONS + 1 ON SERIALIZATION
JerryRigEverything 	How devices are actually built. Watch critically; it's not a model for how to handle
an attendee's device. ASSIGNED: 2 TEARDOWNS
Louis Rossmann 	The why behind right to repair, plus diagnostic thinking way above workshop
level. You're not learning to microsolder. You're learning to think like a technician.
ASSIGNED: 1 DIAGNOSTIC + 1 INDUSTRY VIDEO
Before you move on, you should be able to: describe how much force a pick takes under
a glued screen · tell adhesive releasing apart from glass starting to crack · narrate
a repair out loud · explain serialization to a non-technical person in under a minute
· recover from a mistake without visibly panicking.

MODULE 4 	2H READING + 1H PRACTICE 	THE MOST SKILLED LEADS WHO FAIL, FAIL HERE
TEACHING OLDER ADULTS
None of this is about capability. Think of it as accommodation, the same way a lecture hall has a microphone.
VISION
Print handouts at 14pt or
bigger. Task lighting at
every station. Bump up the
device's own text size first,
and do it with them so they
can do it again at home.
HEARING
Face the person when you
talk. Lower your pitch instead
of raising your volume;
shouting raises pitch and
makes it worse. Never talk
from behind someone.
FINE MOTOR
Tremor and arthritis are
common, and people
usually don't mention them.
Reverse tweezers hold
without grip. Stable surface,
elbow support, both hands
braced. Never rush a hand.
MEMORY
New sequences take longer;
judgment is fine. One step
at a time, written steps to
take home, and explain why
each step exists. People
hold onto reasons way
better than sequences.
ANXIETY
Twenty years of being
told they're "bad with
computers." Some have
broken something and been
blamed for it. It's the biggest
barrier in the room, and
it's entirely psychological.
THE DISCUSSION-BASED MODEL. TAA DOES NOT LECTURE. EVER.
Open with their problem, not your topic.
"What's the thing about your phone that
annoys you most? Go around." Everyone talks
once in the first five minutes, and now you
have the room's real agenda.
Ask, don't tell. "Why do you think the
manufacturer glued this in instead of using
clips?" starts a better right-to-repair
conversation than any speech you could
give.
Teach toward what they said. If four people
said "storage full," that's your lead topic
today, whatever your plan was.
Let them answer each other. An explanation
from a peer lands harder than one from an
expert, and it turns attendees into volunteers.
Close with commitments: "what are you
going to try this week?"

MODULE 4 · 4.3 TO 4.5 	SMALL WORDING CHANGES DECIDE WHO COMES BACK
LANGUAGE RULES & HARD MOMENTS
DON'T SAY 	SAY INSTEAD
"It's easy" "It takes practice. Everybody fumbles this at
first."
"You just tap there" 	"Tap there." Delete the word "just" entirely.
"Let me do it" 	"Try it. I'll talk you through it."
"No, not that one" 	"Close. Try the one right above it."
"I already explained that" 	Explain it again, differently, with no comment
"For someone your age,
you're doing great" "You're doing great"
"Your phone is really old" "Your phone is a few generations back. Here's
what that means for you."
"Just" is the most common condescension in tech teaching.
"You just go to settings" tells a struggling person that the thing
beating them is trivial. Cut it from your vocabulary at the table.
THE HARD MOMENTS
"I'm too old for this." Don't argue and don't over-reassure; both feel dismissive. Point at
the evidence: "You've used this phone for three years without anyone showing you how.
That's not someone who can't learn. That's someone nobody ever taught."
The person who was scammed. Stop what you're doing. No alarm, and never "how did
you fall for that." These scams are built by professionals to be convincing. Help them
change the password and turn on two-factor, their hands on the phone, not yours, and
give them reportfraud.ftc.gov.
The dominant talker. "Good, hold that thought, I want to come back to it. Margaret, what
did you find?" Then call on the quiet people by name. And consider recruiting the talker;
that energy is useful in the right seat.
The person who came for something else. Loneliness, or a family that stopped answering
tech questions. That's legitimate. The digital literacy track exists partly for this, and a
chapter that only counts repairs misses most of its impact.
W H A T N E V E R H A P P E N S A T A T A A W O R K S H O P
Nobody gets laughed at · no question gets treated as obvious · no device gets taken
without asking ("May I?", and hand it back screen facing them) · nobody gets told
they're too old · no talking about attendees where they can hear · no accounts, photos,
or messages get opened · no passcode gets written down · no device leaves the room.

MODULE 5 	2H READING + 1H PRACTICE 	KEEPING YOUR HANDS OFF OTHER PEOPLE'S WORK
THE ESCALATION LADDER
WHY FAILURE IS THE CURRICULUM
Someone who watched you replace a battery learned that
battery replacement is
possible. Someone who replaced
one themselves, badly, slowly, with two mistakes and one
small panic, learned that they can replace a battery. Only
the second one changes what they do after they go home.
Anyone can follow twelve steps. A repairer is someone
who can handle step seven going wrong. Recovery
is the real skill, which is why people have to be
allowed to fail in front of you, inside the safe boundary
the practice-device policy draws.
THE FAILURE MODE TO WATCH FOR
Jumping straight to rung 7 because it's faster. It
is faster. It's also the whole reason a workshop
can feel busy and teach nobody anything.
" J U S T F I X I T F O R M E "
"I could do that in five minutes, but then next time you'd
be right back here. Give me twenty and you'll be able
to do it yourself." The line is capability, not willingness.
Someone who physically can't hold tweezers gets
help, narrated clearly, with written steps to take home.
R1 	WAIT. Say nothing. Count to ten. Most fumbling sorts itself out, and the sorting-
out is where the learning happens. New volunteers jump in after about two
seconds. Train yourself out of it.
R2 	ASK A QUESTION. "What's it doing?" · "What do you think is holding it?" · "What
did the guide say about that corner?"
R3 	DESCRIBE. "The tab is on the left edge, about an inch from the corner."
R4 	POINT. At the spot. Still don't touch the device.
R5 	DEMONSTRATE ON A DIFFERENT DEVICE. Show the motion on your own practice
unit, then hand theirs back. This solves most of what's left, and almost nobody
uses it enough.
R6 	GUIDED TOUCH. "May I put my hand over yours so you can feel the pressure?"
Ask first. Their hand stays on the tool.
R7 	YOU TAKE OVER. Only for an active safety risk, or when going further will kill the
device and the attendee asked you to. Announce it, narrate it, and hand it back
the second it's past the hard part.

MODULE 5 · 5.3 TO 5.5 	A TWO-HOUR SESSION. ADAPT IT, BUT KEEP THE SHAPE.
RUN-OF-SHOW & THE PRACTICE-DEVICE POLICY
T-45 Setup: stations, lighting, cords taped down, fireproof container out,
tools counted in, walker clearance checked
T-15 Volunteer huddle. First-workshop volunteers get paired, never solo.
The Lead makes the stop call.
0:00 Doors. Greeter gets names, sign-in, acknowledgment form, and "what
brought you in today?"
0:10 Safety briefing. Every workshop. No exceptions. Two minutes, and
nobody touches a tool before it happens.
0:12
Go-around: your name, plus the thing that annoys you most about
your device. Write the list up where everyone can see it. That's your
agenda.
0:20 Station work. 2 to 4 attendees per instructor. Escalation ladder in
effect.
1:00 Cross-pollinate: one person from each station shares what surprised
them. Five minutes.
1:40 Hard stop on new disassembly. Close, test, account for everything. An
open device at the end of a session goes home broken.
1:50 Close-out: one thing you learned, one thing you'll try at home.
Announce the next date.
2:00 Teardown: tools counted out, batteries contained, room left better
than you found it. That's how you keep the venue.

Don't negotiate with yourself on this. The pressure to say yes will come from a nice
person who really wants their phone fixed. That's exactly the situation the rule exists for.
Why this exists, said plainly: a chapter lead is a volunteer who read a handbook. Fifty
of those in fifty towns opening strangers' phones on demand gets you bricked devices,
lost family photos, and eventually an injury. Any one of those with TAA's name on it
costs us the relationships that make this whole thing run. Practice devices remove
nearly all of that risk and almost none of the learning. That's a rare trade, and we take it.

PART 7 · 7.3 TO 7.6 	YOUR JOB ISN'T TO EXIST. IT'S TO RUN A WORKSHOP EVERY MONTH.
VOLUNTEERS, DEVICES, REPORTING
RECRUITING VOLUNTEERS
Your best source is your own attendees,
especially anyone who starts helping
the person next to them without being
asked. Recruit those people on the spot.
→ Retired engineers, technicians, electricians. Every
town has them, and they're under-used
→ Students who need service hours (they need the
most coaching on Module 4)
→ The library's volunteer pool, makerspaces, radio
clubs
Nobody works a station alone on their first workshop.
Nobody handles batteries without Module 1. And check
whether your venue requires background checks;
many do, and it's their process, not yours to waive.
SOURCING PRACTICE DEVICES
→ Ask your attendees. The single best source. Every
household has dead phones in a drawer, and people
love having somewhere for them to go
→ TAA's donated inventory (ask; logistics vary by
distance)
→ Library and town IT retirements
→ Local repair shops' scrap bins, which often turns
into a referral relationship both ways
→ E-waste collection events (ask the organizer first)
Wipe every donated device or pull the drive
before it becomes teaching material. Skip
this and you'll eventually hand someone
a laptop with a stranger's tax returns on it.
REPORTING
Post-workshop report within seven days,
every workshop. It takes ten minutes and
it's a charter condition. It builds the dataset
that proves the program works and gives
TAA the numbers sponsors ask about.
Report the bad ones too. A session where four
people showed up and a repair failed is more
useful to us than a session you never wrote
up. We're not grading you on attendance.

PART 9 	THE GATE IS REAL, AND PASSING IT MEANS SOMETHING
CERTIFICATION SUBMISSION
SUBMIT ALL SIX ITEMS TOGETHER
01 	Knowledge check: all 68 questions of Part 8, in writing, in your own words
02 	Repair video, 5 to 8 minutes: one complete repair on a practice device, filmed
straight through, hands visible. Mat in use, wrist strap clipped, fireproof container in
frame for battery work, and the device powering on at the end. If something goes
wrong, keep filming. A mistake handled calmly is better evidence than a flawless run.
03 	Mock lesson video, 10 minutes: teach one genuine beginner. We count how many
times you touched their device, and whether "just," "simply," or "easy" showed up. A
technically perfect lesson where you did the work for them fails. That's the point.
04 	Checkpoint evidence: the photos and short write-ups from all five module
checkpoints
05 	Your referral list, completed for your town
06 	Chapter plan, one page: venue and contact, recurring date, inventory, container and
disposal point, your Co-Lead prospect, and your first three workshop topics
Also required: at least six completed iFixit guides across four device classes, tracked on your
account, plus the foundation reading, from Tools Best Practices to the swollen-battery wiki.
TAA SENDS BACK ONE OF FOUR RESULTS
CERTIFIED
Charter issued, provisional status. Schedule workshop 1.
CERTIFIED WITH NOTES
Approved, with specific fixes we read back to you before
workshop 1.
RESUBMIT
Common, and not a rejection. It's usually the mock lesson;
the escalation ladder is genuinely hard.
NOT APPROVED
We'll tell you exactly why, including when it's a fit issue
instead of a skill issue.
